I finally did something I'd been meaning to do for years: pulled every run I've ever logged on Strava and turned it into a real dashboard. Not a screenshot of Strava's own charts — an actual interactive site, built from my own export of the data, with filters, tooltips, and a few visualizations Strava doesn't give you out of the box.

**[Check out the dashboard here.](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/ffcded54-3d51-4cb2-839f-907d20bac4f5)**

The numbers, going back to my first logged run in June 2019: 711 runs, 3,922 miles, 544 hours moving, and a little over 164,000 feet of climbing — enough to summit Everest from sea level close to six times over. 2022 was the biggest year by a wide margin, 1,011 miles, which tracks — that's the year I was deep into marathon training and had fewer excuses not to run.

The part I had the most fun building was the world map. I've GPS-tracked runs in 29 different places, and laid out on an actual map, the data basically draws my last few years for me without me having to say a word: a thick cluster of runs in Chicago that stops cold in early 2023, and a new cluster in Columbus that picks up right around the same time — because that's when I moved. Under that are all the smaller dots: San Diego, where I go often enough that it shows up as a real secondary base, plus one-off runs from work trips and vacations — Rome, Barcelona, Malta, Moab, a few ski towns. There's a route heatmap for my three most-run cities too, so you can see literally which streets I've worn down the most.

I also broke things out by shoe and by workout type, mostly out of curiosity. Turns out I'm pretty loyal to Hoka once I find a model I like, and the vast majority of my miles fall into the "easy / recovery" bucket rather than anything structured — which is either a training philosophy or a mild character flaw, depending on who you ask.

One honest data point, since I wrote about training for the 2025 Columbus Marathon on this blog: I didn't beat my PR. My first Columbus Marathon in 2023 came in at 3:47, and the 2025 race came in at 4:13 — about 26 minutes slower. The data's the data. I'm not going to pretend otherwise, and I'm also not going to overanalyze why from a spreadsheet — some things you just go run again. Which, per the dashboard, I've already started doing.

If you want to dig into your own slice of it — pick a year, look at pace trends, see where the mileage went — the dashboard is fully interactive, right down to the map. Have a look and let me know what you find.
